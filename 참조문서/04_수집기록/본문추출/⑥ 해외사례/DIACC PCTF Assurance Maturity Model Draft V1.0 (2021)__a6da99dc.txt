2. Introduction 76 
It is essential that Participants in a digital ecosystem have a way to evaluate the 77 
robustness and trustworthiness of transactions within that ecosystem. In order to do so, 78 
Participants must share a common vocabulary that describes the level of confidence 79 
they can associate with an Entity or transaction, as well as a common way in which to 80 
determine that level of confidence. 81 
In the Pan-Canadian Trust Framework™ (PCTF), a Level of Assurance (LoA) 82 
represents the level of confidence an Entity may place in the processes and other 83 
conformance criteria defined in any given component of the PCTF. Levels of Assurance 84 
are elemental in creating networks of trust. Levels of Assurance models only work if all 85 
Participants in a digital ecosystem are able to interpret them consistently. It is therefore 86 
critical that all Participants in an ecosystem agree upon a minimum set of criteria for 87 
each Level of Assurance. Only then will a Relying Party in that ecosystem be able to 88 
properly evaluate the risks inherent in a relationship or transaction, and the Level of 89 
Assurance that can be placed in Participants, Credentials, and those transactions. The 90 
components of the PCTF describe the detailed conformance criteria that should be used 91 
to evaluate such Levels of Assurance in the context of a given PCTF component. This 92 
document provides guidance regarding how to use those criteria in order to properly 93 
classify Levels of Assurance. 94

2.1.1 The Whistleblower: An Illustrative Use Case 131 
Consider, for example, the case of a fictitious whistleblower. In our example someone 132 
has learned that a group they are a member of is engaging in unlawful, immoral, or 133 
even a life-endangering activity. They need to make this activity known in order to 134 
preserve the integrity of the group and protect the lives of those impacted. They must 135 
remain anonymous. Exposing their identity might result in their dismissal from the 136 
group, removing their ability to surface the wrongdoing and, more importantly, 137 
preventing them from mitigating the harm it causes. In addition, exposure of their 138 
identity may cause irreparable damage to their career or ability to earn income. In 139 
extreme cases it might also put their lives, or the lives of their friends and family, in 140 
danger. 141 
In a typical digital transaction, a key question is, "Do I know this person (or Entity) and 142 
are they whom they claim to be?". However, in this case our Subject will never divulge 143 
their true identity. Thus, once they have made initial contact and proven their validity as 144 
an insider (e.g., by providing verifiable information that proves their provenance) that 145

Once we trust them, we need to ensure we can trust things such as whether their 147

Even though a transaction with these characteristics is likely sufficiently robust for this 165 
whistleblower case, the fact that it uses a self-asserted identity – which the criteria 166 
classify as an LoA 1-level identity – will cause the entire transaction to be evaluated as 167 
LoA 1 under this assurance scheme. Thus, it would likely be assessed by a Relying 168 
Party as untrustworthy. Since this scheme delivers a single Level of Assurance, the 169 
Relying Party would have no way of knowing that it is sufficiently robust for the use 170 
case, nor would they know why it was assessed as LoA1. They would be unaware that 171 
the transaction was authenticated at LoA 3, and it was evaluated overall at LoA 1 solely 172 
due to the fact the identity was self-asserted. 173 
2.1.2 The need for an evolutionary approach 174 
There are many use cases where a weakest-link scheme such as this is insufficient. 175 
However, weakest-link schemes such as these have been widely adopted and cannot 176 
be ignored. Thus, it is clear the PCTF requires an evolutionary approach that offers 177 
Relying Parties a range of assurance options that enable them to begin with today's 178 
widely used models and evolve as needed to address their own risks and requirements. 179

The Pan-Canadian Trust Framework Assurance Maturity Model consists of three, 182 
interoperable models of maturity: 183 
1. Traditional Levels of Assurance. 184 
2. Discrete Levels of Assurance. 185 
3. Vectors of Trust. 186 
While this represents an evolutionary model, it should be noted that there is no 187 
requirement for practitioners to adopt any of the models. Practitioners may begin at any 188 
point in the model, may move directly to any other model, and need not have previously 189 
adopted any other model. 190 
3.1 Model 1: Traditional Levels of Assurance 191 
(Traditional LoA) 192 
The Traditional Levels of Assurance model originally described earlier becomes the 193 
basis for maturity Model 1. This represents the most broadly implemented assurance 194 
scheme at the time of this writing and provides an on-ramp to the PCTF for those who 195 
have currently implemented this scheme. 196

As demonstrated in the application of the Traditional Levels of Assurance to the 224

3.2 Model 2: Discrete Levels of Assurance (Discrete 237 
LoA) 238 
Model 2 of the maturity model introduces Discrete Levels of Assurance. Discrete LoA 239 
breaks the bonds between the individual components of the assurance scheme and 240 
enables independent evaluation of authentication assurance levels (AAL), identity 241 
assurance levels (IAL), and credential assurance levels (CAL). It places more emphasis 242 
on the Relying Party's requirements through increased attention to threat-risk analysis. 243 
It also aligns the conformance criteria with the Relying Party's identified risks and 244 
ensures they are evaluated in a manner commensurate with those risks. 245 
 246 
Figure 6: Discrete Levels of Assurance 247 
Evaluation of Discrete Levels of Assurance is as follows: 248 
1. Relevant conformance criteria in each component are evaluated to determine 249 
which LoA has been achieved for each criterion. 250 
2. The Level of Assurance for a specific component is equal to the lowest LoA 251 
assessed across all of the relevant criteria for that PCTF component. 252 
3. The Level of Assurance for each component is reported independently. 253 
Characterization of Discrete Levels of Assurance remains consistent with the 254 
characteristics outlined in Figure 4, though each individual component is characterized 255 
independently of the others. Though it may appear in Figure 6 that assessment of a 256 
Verified Person and Verified Organization are combined in the assessment of an IAL, 257 
an Entity would be either a Verified Person or a Verified Organization, though not both. 258 
Thus, only one of those would be applicable to an Entity and would be considered 259 
during evaluation of its IAL. 260

The Discrete LoA approach is very similar to the Traditional LoA approach, though 263

also places emphasis on the evaluation of risks in the context of each individual Relying 267

Though the Discrete LoA approach provides much more acuity and flexibility than a 283 
Traditional LoA approach, like the Traditional LoA scheme it is an aggregate approach 284 
that does not specifically identify the rationale for an assigned Level of Assurance, the 285 
weakest link, nor the specific strengths. For example, even though in our whistleblower 286

The Vectors of Trust approach to assurance: 306 
• Enables the Relying Party to assess risk with better acuity than Traditional LoA 307 
and Discrete LoA schemes. 308 
• Enables Relying Parties to decide which components are relevant to their risks or 309 
request only the components they need. 310 
• Is flexible in its implementation. 311 
• Can be mapped to Traditional LoA and Discrete LoA schemes for interoperability. 312 
• Is not subject to inconsistencies in evaluation of LoA levels or to LoA level drift. 313 
• Does not require recalibration to account for new technologies and best 314 
practices. 315 
• Provides insight into a transaction's specific strengths and weaknesses. 316 
• Is extensible as the PCTF grows. 317 
• Is designed to evolve with new technologies and best practices. 318 
While the PCTF provides a set of baseline vectors and attributes, Relying Parties and 319 
Participants can negotiate specific attributes that best meet their needs and address 320 
their specific risks. This may include additional vector components, and some, all, or 321 
none of the baseline vector components. 322

conformance has been assessed against the criteria for the PCTF Notice & Consent 413 
component. For example, an assessment of “conforming” would be expressed as “N1”. 414 
The value “N0” may be used to document an assessment of “not conforming” to the 415 
Notice & Consent component. If an assessment of conformance against the Notice & 416 
Consent component has not been performed, a “N” component must not be included in 417 
a vector. 418 
3.3.3.7 Infrastructure (Technology & Operations) (“S”) 419 
The Infrastructure (Technology & Operations) dimension of the PCTF Vectors of Trust 420 
should express that conformance has been assessed against the criteria for the PCTF 421 
Infrastructure (Technology & Operations) component. For example, an assessment of 422 
“conforming” would be expressed as “S1”. The value “S0” may be used to document an 423 
assessment of “not conforming” to the Infrastructure (Technology & Operations) 424 
component. If an assessment of conformance against the Privacy component has not 425 
been performed, a “S” component must not be included in a vector. 426
