Abstract 
This paper explores digital public infrastructure (DPI), defined as shared 
digital systems that are secure and interoperable and that can support the 
inclusive delivery of and access to public and private services across 
society. Governments play a pivotal role in designing, developing and 
managing DPI, as well as in leveraging DPI systems to increase efficiency 
and coherence in the implementation of digital government policies. Key 
components of DPI include digital identity, payments, data sharing, digital 
post, and core government data registries. Building robust DPI requires 
addressing governance challenges such as funding, public-private 
collaboration, and robust safeguards including for privacy and security. As 
DPI becomes a global priority, enhanced cross-border co-operation is also 
vital to address issues like resilience and to ensure effective DPI across 
diverse economic contexts.

Executive summary 
This paper explores the development and impact of digital public infrastructure (DPI) on digital government 
and outlines key recommendations for policymakers. DPI refers to a set of shared, secure, and 
interoperable digital systems designed to support broad access to public and private services. Examples 
include digital identity, data sharing systems, digital payments and digital post and notifications. These DPI 
systems act as common digital building blocks that underpin government processes and services and 
enable digital government transformation at scale. Governments play a central role in DPI’s design, 
development, deployment, and oversight, with some systems tailored specifically to public sector needs, 
others benefiting both the public and private sectors, and some capable of operating across borders. 
DPI offers substantial socioeconomic advantages. It can generate savings and efficiency gains for the 
public sector and for the private sector. Alongside economic gains, reliable DPI can promote inclusivity, 
service resilience, security, and user satisfaction, providing essential infrastructure for efficient, scalable, 
and innovative services. The main benefits are: 
• 
Efficiency: When public sector institutions and private service providers can rely on established 
tools and platforms, they can redirect resources to more valuable tasks and create economies of 
scale, saving time and money.  
• 
Interoperability: DPI can promote interoperability by providing common components and 
facilitating standardised approaches and methods for designing and providing services, for sharing 
and accessing data, and for communicating and collaborating among and across teams, 
departments, organisations, and borders.  
• 
User experience and inclusion: Robust and reliable DPI supports a better user experience with 
digital public services and can help make services more inclusive by providing tools that assist 
public sector institutions and private service providers in tailoring the services more efficiently to 
user needs.  
• 
Scalability: As the volume and complexity of data and digital public services continue to grow; 
scalability becomes a critical consideration. Reusable digital building blocks are designed to scale 
up along with increased demand and to prevent lock-in effects, ensuring that the ability to offer 
quality services can adapt to evolving needs rapidly without significant rework, investment or 
impact on service continuity.  
• 
Resilience: Countries with robust digital foundations are better equipped to navigate crises and 
deliver essential services. This was demonstrated during the COVID-19 pandemic, when some 
countries were able to implement key services and support more rapidly in response to emerging 
challenges by leveraging DPI.  
• 
Innovation: Having access to a foundation of reusable digital tools and platforms allows public 
sector institutions and private service providers to build on existing solutions rather than start from 
scratch, and to experiment with new approaches and technologies more efficiently.

Country experiences show that the implications of DPI for digital government go beyond technology 
adoption. It is essential to consider how DPI is managed and governed by governments, how it is funded, 
what public-private partnerships are required for its design, development, deployment, and use, and what 
safeguards are needed for trusted and sustainable DPI. This includes ensuring that DPI does not lead to 
exclusion, and addressing challenges such as privacy concerns, security vulnerabilities, and issues around 
dependence and resilience.  
Policy recommendations 
To ensure the successful design, development and implementation of DPI, countries should consider the 
following actions: 
• 
Adopt a strategic framework to effectively govern and manage the country’s DPI. This could 
cover digital identity, digital payments, core data registries, data-sharing systems, digital 
notifications, digital post, and single digital gateways.  
• 
Promote multi-stakeholder collaboration on DPI to enable transparency, innovation, and 
scalability, including potential cross-sectoral and cross-border adoption. 
• 
Ensure sustainable DPI through strategic investment and funding across DPI design, 
development, operation, and maintenance, while considering the transition from legacy systems 
and aligning with policy goals such as environmental sustainability and resilience. Robust planning, 
prioritisation, and oversight are critical to maximise long-term benefits and return on investment. 
• 
Adopt a human-centred approach to DPI by prioritising individuals’ rights, needs, and 
preferences. This also includes implementing robust safeguards, such as privacy-by-design and 
digital security measures, robust data governance frameworks, and ethical standards.   
• 
Engage in international digital co-operation to strengthen peer learning and share valuable 
solutions, including digital public goods, to support the development of DPI across countries and 
regions with different contexts and economic development levels.

While global attention to Digital public infrastructure (DPI) is growing, developing DPI has long been a 
priority among OECD countries as part of their digital government efforts. DPI, as defined by the G20, 
refers to a set of shared digital systems that are secure and interoperable and can support the inclusive 
delivery of and access to public and private services at societal scale (G20, 2023[1]). In most OECD 
countries, governments play a pivotal role in designing, developing, deploying and overseeing DPI. Some 
DPI are designed specifically for public sector needs, while others adopt a whole-of-society approach, 
benefiting both public and private sectors. A related concept is digital public goods (DPGs), which refers 
to the use of open-source software, open data, open AI models, open standards, and open content to 
facilitate re-use across organisations, sectors, levels of government, and countries (United Nations, 
2024[2]). Elements of a country’s DPI can be designed to incorporate DPGs (e.g. open source and open 
standards) (Digital Public Goods Alliance; GIZ, 2022[3]). 
DPI can offer significant socioeconomic advantages. In Norway, the joint national solutions for geospatial 
data provided by the National Mapping Authority generate yearly benefits to the country’s private sector of 
up to 13 billion NOK (approximately 1 billion EUR) (Kartverket, 2024[4]). In Estonia, the use of digital 
signatures has saved up to 2% of GDP (e-Estonia, 2019[5]). Additionally, it is estimated that DPI can 
increase the GDP of low- and middle-income countries by between 1 and 1.4 percentage points by 2030 
(UNDP, 2023[6]). While economic benefits are a key factor for investing in DPI, other major opportunities 
include greater inclusion and a better user experience through improved public and private services, as 
well as more reliable, secure and resilient access to and use of those services.   
In recent years, DPI has featured in discussions at the United Nations (UN) and G20, particularly in relation 
to advancing inclusive digital transformation in developing countries. Building on the results of the 2023 
G20 Indian Presidency1, and inspired by India’s IndiaStack, several initiatives have emerged to frame and 
promote DPI in developing countries. Notably, the UN Global Digital Compact identifies DPI as an enabler 
to close digital divides and accelerate actions and progress towards the achievement of the Sustainable 
Development Goals (SDGs) (United Nations, 2024[2]). Furthermore, the UN has developed the Universal 
DPI Safeguards Framework, which establishes guiding principles and practices for safe and inclusive DPI. 
Universities and non-profit organisations, such as the University College London and Co-Develop, also 
contribute to the global dialogue on DPI governance and implementation.  
DPI drives digital government transformation at scale by providing common building blocks that support 
government processes and services in the digital era. Discussions around DPI began over a decade ago 
with the idea of evolving 'Government 2.02' into a 'Government as a Platform' model, a cornerstone of the 
OECD Digital Government Policy Framework (OECD, 2020[7]). The rising momentum of DPI underscores 
the need for stronger international digital co-operation. While the concept has been around for years, it has 
only recently gained broader attention. Additionally, there is growing demand for cross-border

The term “Government as a Platform” is a type of governance model where governments provide digital 
infrastructure, services, and frameworks that enable public and private entities to build upon and deliver 
services more efficiently to citizens, fostering collaboration and innovation. It was coined more than a 
decade ago and reflects a desire to transform government structures by designing and delivering 
government services enabled by a core set of reusable digital components (O’Reilly, 2011[8]; OECD, 
2020[7]). With this approach, governments can equip service providers (including those outside the public 
sector and in other countries) with resources to create innovative applications and services that 
governments or individual teams cannot develop alone, benefiting both economies and society (OECD, 
2024[9]). DPI is a central element of implementing this vision (Figure 1).

overseeing DPI as part of their digital government strategies. This chapter looks at examples of DPI

• 
DPI for security and trust, including common systems for digital identity and digital payments. 
•

systems.  
•

DPI for security and trust 
Digital identity  
Digital identity is a core digital public infrastructure, facilitating secure and efficient access to services 
across both public and private sectors, and increasingly across borders. In line with the OECD 
Recommendation on the Governance of Digital Identity, well-governed digital identity systems can 
streamline access to essential services, promote greater inclusivity, and enhance economic opportunities, 
including for marginalised groups. One of the key benefits of digital identity lies in its potential to improve 
user experience, security and efficiency through unified authentication methods like single sign-on or 
federated identity solutions (OECD, 2023[13]; OECD, 2024[14]). Countries are also increasingly adopting 
digital identity wallets and credentials, which will allow individuals and organisations to securely share 
verified attributes and credentials and more easily access services both online and offline (OECD, 2024[9]). 
Digital identity is highly linked to secure and trusted digital payments, data and information sharing, and 
communication and public service interaction.

Digital payments 
Combined with secure digital identity frameworks and interoperable base registries (core data registries), 
a unified and secure government-to-person (G2P) payment system can support more security and 
accuracy in high-volume transactions like social cash benefits, which represent up to 20% of GDP in some 
OECD countries (OECD, 2024[15]) or tax refunds and emergency cash transfers. One example is 
Denmark’s NemKonto (Box 2). Another is Brazil’s Pix system, which is an instant payment system 
managed by Brazil’s central bank that enables people, companies, and governmental entities to send or 
receive instant payment transfers. In addition, a consistent, integrated person-to-government (P2G) digital 
payment system for public services can simplify transactions for citizens and businesses to manage 
payments such as taxes and fees. This reduces administrative complexity, enhances transparency, and 
improves service efficiency (OECD, 2024[9]). One example is Italy’s PagoPA (Box 2). Other relevant 
examples in this area include standardised e-invoicing, such as the mandatory use of the PEPPOL 
standard in the EU, and e-procurement systems. 
Box 2. Systems for G2P payments in Denmark and P2G payments in Italy 
Denmark’s NemKonto

NemKonto system makes payments to the person's Danish personal registration number (CPR-

the system more secure. It also means that people do not have to contact several different authorities 
if they change bank accounts. People simply get the new account assigned as their NemKonto, and 
payments will automatically be deposited to the new account. For companies, the payments are made 
to a company’s identification number in the Danish Central Business Register (CVR-number) for the

Italy’s national payment platform PagoPa

DPI for data and information sharing 
Base registries (core data registries) 
Base registries, also known as core databases, foundational registries or core data registries, are 
authoritative sources of key data elements or entities that are fundamental for various administrative or 
governmental processes and services. These registries typically contain essential information about 
individuals, organisations, locations, or assets, and serve as a reliable and centralised source of verified 
information. They form the backbone of DPI and play a crucial role in enabling efficient and effective public 
service delivery. Examples of base registries include registries and databases of people, companies, 
licenses, buildings, locations, roads and vehicles. These are often managed by different public sector 
organisations such as tax authorities, company offices, land registers, statistical agencies or environmental 
agencies.   
Box 3. Brazil’s Cadastro Único 
Cadastro Único is a government-based registry used to support low-income households in Brazil. It 
enables federal and local governments to better understand the country’s vulnerable population by 
recording information such as address, family composition, education, employment, income status, 
disability, among other details. The registry is the main reference used by the Brazilian State to select 
and include low-income families in federal programmes, granting benefits from initiatives such as the 
Bolsa Família Programme (conditional cash transfer), Pé de Meia (educational incentive), Tarifa Social 
de Energia Elétrica (electricity), Auxílio Gás (cooking gas), Minha Casa Minha Vida Programme 
(housing), among others. Additionally, it serves as a criterion for selecting beneficiaries of programmes 
offered by state and municipal governments. Currently, more than 40 federal programmes and more 
than 2,000 municipal and state programmes and policies use information from the registry.

Data sharing  
Effective data sharing systems, with appropriate safeguards including a risk-based approach to privacy 
and security, are critical for creating better and more joined-up public services and interoperability, 
facilitating the implementation of the once-only principle to reduce administrative burden. It is essential for 
enhancing service quality and promoting innovation through data-driven insights. Moreover, these systems 
have the potential to foster partnerships between the public and private sectors and civil society to co-
create services, driving societal progress and economic growth. Examples of existing data sharing systems 
include Estonia’s X-tee data exchange layer, the United Kingdom’s National Data Library, Japan’s Co-
operation Network System for Personal Information, and Spain’s National Data Intermediation Platform 
(PID) (Box 4).  
Box 4. Data sharing systems in Spain, Japan, and the United Kingdom 
Spain’s National Data Intermediation Platform (PID) 
Spain’s National Data Intermediation Platform, known as the PID, is a brokerage system enabling 
competent authorities in Spain to access data services from authoritative sources, thereby obtaining 
relevant information related to public service users. The platform includes an authorisation process to 
verify that there is a public interest and lawful basis for an authority to access the data, in compliance 
with the GDPR. The PID is provided by the Secretariat-General for Digital Administration of the Ministry 
for Digital Transformation and Public Function.  
Japan’s Co-operation Network System for Personal Information 
The Digital Agency of Japan manages the Co-operation Network System (NWS) for Personal 
Information, which is used to exchange personal information associated with the Individual Number 
between government agencies and other organisations, as stipulated by the Individual Number Act. 
This system allows citizens to avoid submitting multiple documents (Certificates of Residence, 
Certificates of Tax, etc.) when completing various administrative procedures. To protect citizen’s 
privacy, only public sector entities have access to the NWS- private citizens and businesses cannot use 
it. Additionally, the NWS does not store personal information itself; it only transmits specific data held 
in a decentralised manner by individual public organisations.  
The UK’s National Data Library 
The National Data Library will unlock the value of public data assets. It will provide simple, secure and 
ethical access to our key public data assets for researchers, policy makers and business – including 
those at the frontier of AI development – and make it easier to find, discover and make connections 
across different datasets. By unlocking public sector data, the National Data Library will support world-
class science and R&D and will lay the foundations for the UK to compete at the global frontier of the 
development of AI, delivering on the Government’s AI action plan. It will also drive forward a modern 
government, enhancing the everyday experience of people and businesses when using public services.

DPI for communication and public service interaction 
The digital age allows the public sector, citizens, and businesses to communicate more easily and securely 
with each other. Sharing sensitive information with citizens and users of public services requires secure 
digital communication as sensitive personal information is often exchanged. At the same time, it is 
important to provide solutions that make these processes more user-friendly and consistent, reaching 
people where they are or through their preferred channels. 
Digital post  
DPI tools such as digital post allow public authorities and private service providers to safely and securely 
send important, often sensitive, information and messages to citizens and businesses through digital 
channels. For example, digital post can be used to send out administrative decisions to individuals, or tax 
declarations. In most countries that deploy digital post, these digital documents and messages are legally 
binding, just like paper documents with official stamps, including deadlines, contracts, and signed 
agreements. Since sensitive information is often shared, security is critical, and people need a secure 
digital identity to access their digital post. Other aspects such as inclusion and accessibility are also 
important. Examples of countries with existing digital post are Sweden (Box 5), Denmark’s digital post and 
Norway’s digital mailbox.  
Box 5. Sweden’s Digital post  
In Sweden, citizens and organisations can receive digital post from public authorities through a secure 
digital mailbox, accessed using electronic identification (e-ID). Currently, there are three digital mailbox 
providers available, and acquiring one is free. The service, called "Mina meddelanden," is a national 
infrastructure enabling safe and secure digital communication with public institutions, orchestrated by 
the Agency for Digital Government (DIGG). 
Various public entities use this platform to send either all or selected types of mail digitally. New senders 
are continually added to the system automatically. Individuals only receive mail from authorities they 
already interact with. To start receiving digital mail, users must choose a digital mailbox provider, 
depending on whether they want to receive mail from both public and private organisations or just public 
entities. This system simplifies communication while ensuring security and convenience for users. 
Source: Agency for Digital Government (2024[18]), Digital post  
Digital notifications 
Common solutions for digital notifications make it easy for public sector institutions to send traditional 
emails, text messages, and physical letters with information to service users, including citizens and 
businesses. For example, these solutions can be used to notify citizens about a progress in their 
administrative procedures or to prompt them to review or update important documents. Several countries 
have developed dedicated, whole-of-government notification services based on open-source solutions, 
such as the United Kingdom (GOV.UK Notify), Canada (GC Notify) (Box 6), the United States (Notify.gov).

Single digital gateways 
Single digital gateways to digital services can serve as digital public infrastructure by facilitating the 
discovery and access of government services while promoting cost-effectiveness by reducing the need for 
individual solutions and platforms. Aligned with the concept of government as a platform, single digital 
gateways can function as centralised “app stores” of government services. They enable the integration of 
digital building blocks –such as digital identity, digital notifications, and payments– as demonstrated by 
Italy's IO app, Denmark's Borger.dk, and Estonia’s mRiik, as well as Spain’s Mi Carpeta Ciudadana and 
Ukraine’s Diia app (Box 7).

Spain’s citizen folder “Mi Carpeta Ciudadana” 
"Mi Carpeta Ciudadana" allows citizens to receive notifications and consult their personal data, 
documentation, appointments, notifications and open files managed by the different public 
administrations in a single point, which will allow them to be updated on their procedures and keep their 
administrative information up to date without having to travel, all from their mobile device. 
It consists of a web portal and a mobile app, both for iOS and Android system, as well as a virtual 
assistant (Ada), currently available only on the web, to help throughout the process, by searching for 
services and typification of incidents, which is in constant learning for the continuous improvement and 
evolution of the service. Mi Carpeta Ciudadana is the first big mobile app released within the Public 
Administrations Digitisation Plan 2021 – 2025. 
Ukraine’s Diia app 
Diia - which is Ukrainian for "action" and an acronym for "state and me" - is a government-issued 
application and portal that provides a new level of interaction between the state and citizens that is 
convenient, seamless, and human-centred. It combines a mobile application with digital documents and 
a portal of public services for citizens and businesses. By the end of the first quarter of 2024, Ukrainians 
could access up to 118 services on the portal and up to forty-five services through the Diia application. 
Through the app, Ukrainian citizens can also store digital documents with the same legal validity as 
their plastic or paper counterparts – forming a digital identity wallet. There are currently nineteen digital 
documents available through the app – including the national ID card, foreign biometric passport, 
driver’s license and more.

Building reliable and effective DPI is a long-term effort. The global experience (including of OECD 
countries) illustrates that the implications of DPI for digital government go beyond technology adoption. 
DPI plays a critical role in enabling improved operations and services, in and beyond the public sector. For 
instance, in Chile, the volume of transactions through DPI represents up to three times the number of 
transactions done by the financial sector (Barros and Painecura Galaz, 2024[20]). Governments have a 
critical role in designing, developing and operating DPI systems that enable digital transformation across 
sectors. Therefore, essential questions include how DPI is managed and governed by governments, how 
it is funded, what public-private partnerships are required for its development, deployment and use, and 
what safeguards are needed for trusted, scalable and sustainable DPI.

Strategic frameworks to govern DPI 
The key role of DPI in driving the digital transformation of governments and other sectors has led some 
countries to establish dedicated frameworks or "stacks" to strategically govern, manage, and use DPI 
components in the public sector and across sectors. These frameworks not only facilitate collaboration 
among different stakeholders and create a shared vision for the design, development, management, and 
promotion of DPI but also play a key role in enhancing cross-sectoral DPI. This is particularly important in 
addressing emergencies such as climate crises or pandemics, where DPI readiness and resilience are 
essential.  
For example, Norway's National Joint Solutions are a set of DPI components overseen by the Norwegian 
Agency for Digital Government (Digdir) (see Box 8). Other countries have adopted comparable 
frameworks, such as Korea’s Digital Platform Government strategy, Sweden’s joint digital government 
infrastructure Ena, Ireland’s Build to Share initiative, Australia’s MyGov, and the Singapore Government 
Tech Stack. There have also been suggestions for developing a common EuroStack for the European 
Union (Bria, 2024[21]). At the global level, the International Telecommunications Union (ITU) is leading the 
GovStack initiative, which focuses on promoting common technical specifications for DPI components and 
other resources, including their GovStack Playbook. 
Box 8. Norway’s national joint solutions 
Launched several years ago, the National Joint Solutions initiative, led by the Norwegian Agency for 
Digital Government (Digdir), has produced 26 accessible national joint solutions, with 19 managed and 
directly provided by Digdir. Eleven solutions are open for use by private entities, and eight are free of 
charge, including the national cadaster register. Notable examples of joint solutions include ID-porten 
(a single-sign-on solution), Varsling (common notification), eBevis & eSignature, Altinn (common 
service platform), Basic data registers (including population register & company register), Styrning av 
tilgang (access control), and Digital postkasse (digital post). Currently, 69% of public agencies in 
Norway use these joint solutions, with 66% expressing positive views on how they contribute to better 
solutions within their own organisations. The National Joint Solutions are managed by the Norwegian 
Agency for Digital Government (Digdir) in collaboration with other relevant government agencies.  
Source: OECD (2024[22]), The Digital Transformation of Norway's Public Sector 
When it comes to developing countries, DPI offers an opportunity to leapfrog progress from a less 
advanced starting point, compared to the trajectory of progress in developed countries with established 
DPI. Developed countries often have a significant number of ageing DPI systems and legacy technology 
that is both expensive and complex to operate and even more expensive to transition from. In contrast, the 
relative technological greenfield of developing countries means they can move more directly and quickly 
to efficient and streamlined solutions. For developing countries to make such progress requires a holistic 
strategic approach that builds firm foundations. It would involve building the fundamental infrastructure 
supporting systems, applications, networks and users, and pairing it with open, interoperable and quality 
data. One example is the work of the Government of India with the IndiaStack, which consists of a wide 
range of common building blocks that are driving the digital transformation of both government and the 
economy, led by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) (Box 9).

Box 9. IndiaStack 
IndiaStack is a comprehensive set of open application programming interfaces (APIs) and digital public 
infrastructure that has revolutionised service delivery in India. It consists of key components such as 
Aadhaar, the world's largest biometric-based digital identity system; the Unified Payments Interface 
(UPI) for real-time, interoperable digital payments, and a series of components for electronic signature

IndiaStack is managed through a collaborative ecosystem involving government bodies, regulatory 
authorities, private entities, and non-profit organisations. The management and development of 
IndiaStack's core components are spearheaded by key institutions under the leadership of the Ministry

Collaboration with the private sector and non-governmental actors 
Fostering public-private collaboration is essential to ensure innovation, scalability, security, and 
accessibility when designing, developing and governing DPI. Given the broad impact of DPI on 
governments, societies, and economies, it is crucial to establish partnerships and promote a dynamic DPI 
ecosystem that includes the public sector, private sector, academia, and non-profit organisations. Such an 
ecosystem and its partnerships must balance the coherent and sustainable management of DPI with its 
application across sectors. This can take several forms, including: 
• 
Co-governance and oversight of DPI: Involving private and non-governmental actors in DPI 
governance can enhance transparency, accountability, and shared ownership of DPI components. 
This includes participation from representatives of the IT sector and relevant civil society 
organisations, which can contribute to a more trusted and sustainable development and use of 
DPI. Examples such as Norway's National Joint Solutions and India's IndiaStack, which are 
managed in collaboration with non-governmental stakeholders (Box 10), highlight the important 
role these actors play in DPI governance. 
• 
Co-development of DPI: The design, development and management of DPI may require 
collaboration with non-government actors, often through public-private partnerships (PPPs). For 
instance, Estonia’s data-sharing platform X-Road is currently managed by the Nordic Institute for 
Interoperability Solutions, a multi-country non-profit organisation that oversees its core 
development, ensuring scalability across countries and sectors. In Denmark, the digital identity 
system MitID (the successor to NemID) is a joint project between public authorities and private 
banks, with shared ownership and funding. 
• 
Cross-sectoral adoption and use of DPI: Crucially, DPI components offer benefits beyond the 
public sector, extending to the wider digital economy. DPI often plays a key role in providing more 
inclusive access to private services. The examples from India, Norway, Denmark, Ireland, and 
Singapore demonstrate how DPI components can be integrated by the private sector to deliver 
services across various sectors.

Box 10. Denmark’s public-private collaboration on digital identity 
In Denmark, the digital identity system MitID is managed through a public-private collaboration involving 
both government entities and private sector partners. The Danish Agency for Digital Government leads 
the public side, ensuring that MitID adheres to national standards and public policy goals. Private sector 
partners, notably banks and other financial institutions, play a crucial role in implementing and operating 
the system. This partnership allows the system to integrate with essential services, including online 
banking, government services, and private platforms. 
The public-private collaboration ensures that MitID is both secure and widely adopted across different 
sectors. By involving private actors, especially banks, the system benefits from their experience in 
managing secure transactions and customer verification. Additionally, the collaboration supports 
innovation while maintaining robust security standards. This cooperative model also helps distribute the 
costs and administrative responsibilities between the public and private sectors.

Investment and funding models 
A key element of successfully implementing DPI is ensuring its support through strategic and sustainable 
investments in digital government. This requires allocating and managing funds not only for the transition 
from legacy systems and the development of DPI but also for DPI operation and maintenance, all while 
aligning with broader policy goals such as environmental sustainability and national resilience. The 
comparison between DPI and physical infrastructure highlights the importance of securing long-term 
development, operation, maintenance, and eventual decommissioning of DPI through multi-year funding, 
alongside potentially shared business models between governments and private sector providers. 
The approach to investing in DPI varies across OECD countries. Many have adopted centralised funding 
models for the development of DPI components. In addition to this, some countries are exploring business 
models that involve contributions from both public and private institutions that use DPI components, 
introducing transaction fees to help finance the operation and maintenance of these systems. For example, 
Sweden, drawing on the experiences of Norway, Finland, and Denmark, has introduced fixed and variable 
fees for entities using DPI components such as digital identity and post (Box 11). 
Given the critical role of DPI, robust investment management models are essential. This includes 
establishing effective planning, prioritisation, and oversight mechanisms that ensure the realisation of 
benefits and a satisfactory return on investment. It also includes the implementation of monitoring tools 
such as DPI-specific key performance indicators (KPIs) that inform the development and operation of DPI 
systems throughout their lifecycle.  
Box 11. Sweden’s funding model for DPI 
DIGG, the agency responsible for digital government in Sweden, has developed a fee and 
compensation model for public actors using DPI, such as digital identity and digital post. The purpose 
of this model is to regulate the fees public entities must pay and the compensation suppliers receive. 
The compensation model consists of two parts: fixed and variable. The fixed portion is designed to 
encourage competition by offering smaller or newer suppliers a consistent pecuniary compensation. 

   23

The variable part is based on transaction volume, where suppliers receive additional compensation if 
they exceed a certain number of annual transactions. Fees paid by public actors are determined by the 
number of transactions in a given period, including administrative costs incurred by DIGG. 
In developing this model, Digg collaborated with experts, public users, and vendors to account for the 
diverse needs of various actors, including large and small authorities, municipalities, and regions. They 
also drew insights from similar systems in Norway, Finland, and Denmark. DIGG plans to evaluate the 
model's effectiveness after some time to address potential changes in the e-identification and digital 
mail markets.

Safeguards 
While DPI offers a wide range of benefits, it also presents challenges and potential risks that need to be 
addressed to ensure the development of effective and trusted systems that contribute to better digital 
societies and leave no one behind. Examples of such risks and challenges include: 
• 
Issues of exclusion: Exclusion arises when DPI is not designed to address the needs of minorities 
or groups with specific requirements, including but not limited to the elderly, migrants, individuals 
with disabilities, or those with limited digital literacy.  
• 
Privacy concerns: Poorly designed DPI may lead to the misuse or unauthorised access to 
personal data, eroding public trust and exposing individuals to risks such as identity theft or 
surveillance. 
• 
Security vulnerabilities: Weak digital security measures in DPI can make societies vulnerable to 
data breaches, cyberattacks, or disruptions of critical services. 
• 
Dependence and resilience: Overreliance on DPI without adequate fallback systems may 
compromise societal resilience during system failures, natural disasters, or cyber incidents. 
To enhance inclusion, privacy, and overall satisfaction with DPI, adopting a human-centred approach is 
essential3. This involves designing, developing, and implementing DPI systems that prioritise the needs, 
rights, experiences, and well-being of individuals (OECD, 2024[9]). Such an approach requires 
understanding and addressing people’s perspectives, preferences, and challenges to create DPI systems 
that are meaningful, accessible, and beneficial. 
Safeguards are also crucial, and international cooperation can play a significant role in their development.  
For example, the UN Universal DPI Safeguards Framework offers a set of principles, processes, and 
practice recommendations to guide the design, deployment, and use of DPI systems. These safeguards 
aim to mitigate potential risks and ensure sustainable, effective implementation (United Nations, 2024[26])). 
Safeguards can take various forms depending on a country’s context and approach to DPI. For instance, 
robust digital security and privacy-by-design and by default measures are important, including mechanisms 
for empowering users with oversight or control over their personal data flowing across DPI components. 
Ethical and trust-related measures are also critical, including promoting fair access, algorithmic 
transparency, adopting strong data governance frameworks, incorporating digital public goods, and 
engaging the public in DPI decision-making (OECD, 2020[7])). Recognising the role of DPI in enabling

Digital public infrastructure (DPI) plays a key role in facilitating digital government transformation and 
advancing the broader digital economy. Countries seeking to design, develop and implement successful 
DPI could consider taking the following steps: 
• 
Adopt a strategic framework to effectively govern and manage the country’s DPI. This could 
cover digital identity, digital payments, core data registries, data sharing systems, digital 
notifications, digital post, and single digital gateways, such as Norway’s National Joint Solutions 
or India’s IndiaStack.  
• 
Promote multi-stakeholder collaboration on DPI to enable transparency, innovation, and 
scalability, including potential cross-sectoral and cross-border adoption, drawing on examples 
such as Denmark’s MitID. 
• 
Ensure sustainable DPI through strategic investment and funding across DPI design, 
development, operation, and maintenance, while considering the transition from legacy systems 
and aligning with policy goals such as environmental sustainability and resilience. Robust 
planning, prioritisation, and oversight are critical to maximise long-term benefits and return on 
investment. 
• 
Adopt and ensure a human-centred approach to DPI by prioritising individuals’ rights, needs, 
and preferences. This also includes implementing robust safeguards, such as privacy-by-design 
and digital security measures, robust data governance frameworks, and ethical standards.   
• 
Engage in international digital co-operation to strengthen peer learning and share valuable 
solutions, including digital public goods, to support the development of DPI across countries and 
regions with different contexts and economic development levels.
